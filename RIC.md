A __Resonant Isolated Conduit (RIC)__ is a "tunnel" of continuously-maintained isolated space designed to support high-volume traffic over large distances without requiring ships to generate and maintain the conduit's __Chronospatial Isolation Ratio (CIR)__ themselves, allowing for much more efficient on-board __isolation drives__.

A __CIR__ describes the degree to which spatial progression has been isolated from temporal progression relative to Typical Spacetime, which has a __CIR__ of 1:1. A __CIR__ of 1:2000 does not mean that time itself is progressing 2,000 times faster or slower for anyone within the isolated space; rather, it means that a moving object requires only 1/2000th as much temporal progression to cover a given spatial distance as it would at the same local velocity through Typical Spacetime. Clocks, biological processes, electronics, and other local processes continue to operate normally.

Ships travelling through a __RIC__ therefore still require conventional propulsion. Most commercial ships use __magnetoplasmadynamic (MPD) propulsion__ powered by on-board fusion generators, while some specialised vessels, particularly maintenance and emergency-response ships, use high-thrust chemical propulsion to achieve much greater velocities over the comparatively short response profiles they are designed for.

The effective rate at which a ship can cover distance through a __RIC__ therefore depends both on the __RIC__'s __CIR__ and the ship's own local velocity. A faster ship travelling through the same __RIC__ will reach its destination sooner than a slower ship.

Ships require a __high-impulse isolation generator (HIIG)__-compatible __isolation drive__ to use a conventional __RIC__, as they must first bring their own isolation field within a safe threshold of the __RIC__ before they can safely enter it. Theoretically, any ship capable of maintaining a __CIR__ within this threshold could freely move between an __RIC__ and Typical Spacetime, though in practice this is strictly reserved for [Open RICs (ORICs)](#Open%20RICs%20\(ORICs\)) for safety reasons.

To understand how a __RIC__ works, we will follow a theoretical ship wanting to use one.

## Synchronisation Lock (Departure)

The process begins for our theoretical ship by entering the __Synchronisation Lock__ while it is __Onboarding__.

__Synchronisation locks__ are "valves" of the wider __RIC__ used for entry and exit. Their __CIR__ can be altered independently of the __RIC__, allowing them to safely expose themselves either to ships experiencing Typical Spacetime or to the much more isolated environment of the __RIC__ proper.

During __Onboarding__, the __synchronisation lock__ has a __CIR__ of 1:1, otherwise referred to as Typical Spacetime. Ships can therefore enter the __synchronisation lock__ without needing to alter their own __CIR__.

__Synchronisation locks__ follow a timetabled schedule and will cycle regardless of how many ships are presently within the lock.

When ready, the __synchronisation lock control tower (SLCT)__ will issue a __remote operation request (ROR)__ to each ship within the lock's boundaries, requesting the transfer of full navigational and isolation-drive control to the __SLCT__. Any ship unwilling to relinquish control will be asked to leave, or forcefully removed if it refuses.

Once the __SLCT__ has control, it will begin charging each ship's __isolation generator impulse capacitors (IGICs)__ if this has not already been done. Ships are free to begin charging their __IGICs__ before entering the lock, and regularly do so to avoid unnecessary delays.

At this point the external status of the lock will be set to __Charging__, and the lock will remain open until the next stage. The __Charging__ stage lasts a finite amount of time, and any ship that is not sufficiently charged by its conclusion will be removed from the lock immediately.

After __Charging__, a __general status request (GSR)__ will be sent to every ship remaining within the lock to confirm readiness to proceed. If a ship responds unready, its captain will speak directly with the __SLCT__ to discuss its status, rectify any issues, provide an ETA, or leave the lock if required to maintain the __RIC__'s schedule. If a ship does not respond at all, it will be removed from the lock.

The __SLCT__ maintains full control over the ship throughout this process, though the ship's captain remains responsible for responding to the __GSR__.

Once all ships are sufficiently charged and have confirmed readiness, the lock will update its status to __Synchronising__, and the __SLCT__ will begin spooling each ship's on-board __isolation drive__.

Shortly after isolation-drive spooling begins, a second __GSR__ will be sent once each drive has reached full spool. This second request confirms that both the ship and its isolation systems remain ready immediately prior to the transition. It is extraordinarily rare for a drive to fail to reach full spool; however, if it does, the at-fault ship will either be put through to discuss the situation with the __SLCT__ or immediately removed depending on the __RIC__'s schedule.

If all ships are charged, spooled, and confirmed ready, the __synchronisation lock__ will update its status to __Impulse__. From this point onward, any vessel attempting to cross the lock's boundaries without authorisation will be immediately __destroyed__ to prevent a VRE.

The __SLCT__ will put each ship's __isolation drive__ into __HIIG mode__, where the drive prepares itself for a very high one-shot burst of energy capable of achieving a momentary __CIR__ far greater than the drive could ordinarily maintain.

Simultaneously, the isolation generators controlling the __synchronisation lock__ itself prepare to rapidly increase the lock's __CIR__ toward that of the __RIC__.

The timing here is absolutely crucial.

The intention is for each ship to momentarily reach a sufficiently high __CIR__ that, as the lock rapidly increases its own isolation, the ship remains safely within the lock's isolation field without crossing an unsafe chronospatial differential and causing a CMCE. Once the lock has reached a sufficiently similar __CIR__ to the __RIC__, the ship can safely remain within the lock's field without maintaining the full isolation state itself.

Thankfully, due to the extreme precautions taken throughout the process, this transition has never failed in a real-world operational scenario. The automated timing systems responsible for orchestrating the transition use some of the most precise clocks and computers that the __ISCIA__ can certify; attempting to perform the impulse sequence manually is considered wholly impractical.

Assuming all has gone well, our ship is now free to enter and travel through the __RIC__.

The __SLCT__ will upload an autopilot path to the ship, accounting for the vessel's propulsion profile, intended velocity, surrounding traffic, and destination. Control will remain locked while the ship travels through the __RIC__, preventing independently-controlled vessels from interfering with the wider traffic stream.

A physical aperture between the __synchronisation lock__ and the __RIC__ proper will open, and the ship will transfer from the lock into the conduit along with the rest of its scheduled traffic. The aperture will then close behind them and the lock will begin preparing for its next cycle.

## RIC Proper

Our journey through the __RIC__ is mostly automated. We can sit back and relax while our ship's normal propulsion carries us through the conduit, though, relatively speaking, we will not be here for especially long.

The whole point of a __RIC__ is that, because it is a permanent installation with effectively no practical mass or volume restrictions comparable to those imposed on a spacecraft, it can maintain __CIRs__ far greater than a lone ship could reasonably sustain.

Most __RICs__ operate at a chronospatial separation of roughly __χ = 24 to 25__, corresponding to __CIRs__ of approximately __1:2.6×10¹⁰ to 1:7.2×10¹⁰__, though this varies substantially depending on the route, age, traffic volume, and infrastructure involved.

One of the most capable __RICs__ in the Immediate Stellar Cluster presently operates at approximately __χ = 25 (1:7.2×10¹⁰)__, while an experimental new approach to isolation generation is being tested with the intention of approaching __χ = 26 (roughly 1:2×10¹¹)__.

Because a ship's transit time depends both on its local velocity and the __RIC__'s __CIR__, two ships travelling through the same conduit may have considerably different journey times. Increasing a conduit from 1:3×10¹⁰ to 1:6×10¹⁰ (raising its χ by ln 2 ≈ 0.69) would, all else being equal, approximately halve the amount of time required for the same ship to cross it.

For a ship assigned a steady acceleration (a) for the first half of the conduit and an equal deceleration for the second, the crossing time is approximately:

$$
  
T\approx2\sqrt{\frac{D}{N\,a}}  
$$

Where (D) is the conduit's length. The synchronisation-lock cycles at either end add to this.

For our theoretical journey between Sol and Alpha Centauri, the __RIC__ operates at approximately __χ = 24.3 (1:3.6×10¹⁰)__. Our actual travel time will therefore depend on the velocity profile assigned to our ship by the __SLCT__, along with acceleration, deceleration, and traffic constraints within the conduit.

As we are a small ship in this example with only a few crew members, our crossing will take a little under an hour, which we will likely spend at our stations or in recreation. A larger vessel, such as one of the major interstellar freighters carrying several hundred crew members, accelerates far more gently. It may spend several hours in the conduit and would instead operate in shifts to maintain the ship throughout transit.

### RIC Structure

Despite commonly being described as a "tunnel", a __RIC__ is not a single structure or a single isolation field extending continuously between two star systems.

Instead, a __RIC__ is constructed from enormous numbers of individual segments centred around physical __RIC rings__.

Each ring contains four specialised isolation generators positioned around the conduit. Together, these generators maintain an isolated field within the ring itself and for some distance in front of and behind it. The fields of adjacent rings overlap, producing a continuous isolated region along the full length of the conduit.

The isolation generators operate continuously but do not blindly maintain a fixed output. Instead, each ring monitors the local __CIR__ and compares it with the safe operating threshold of the surrounding __RIC__. If the field begins to drift beyond this threshold, energy is passed through the generators to bring it back into alignment.

This creates a constant "topping-off" behaviour across the whole conduit rather than requiring any single generator to maintain isolation over interstellar distances.

The rings themselves are not completely stationary. Every ring is fitted with a suite of __Hall-effect reaction-control thrusters__, allowing it to perform small adjustments to its position and orientation.

This is necessary because a __RIC__ does not connect two perfectly stationary points in space. Stellar systems move relative to one another, while the individual rings themselves are subject to gravitational effects and positional drift. The conduit therefore behaves as an enormous controlled formation rather than a rigid physical structure, with individual rings continuously performing station-keeping to preserve the geometry of the wider __RIC__.

### Alignment and Termini

A __RIC__ always follows the shortest direct path between its two termini.

Its __CIR__ can only be sustained far from gravitating bodies (see [[Isolation#38. Isolation Boundaries and the Environmental Limit|Isolation Boundaries]]), so its termini lie in the outer reaches of their systems, typically some 250 to 700 AU from the star. Ships make the inner-system leg to and from a terminus under their own isolation drives.

As the stars drift, the termini slide and the conduit swings to follow them. Over a 25-light-year conduit, a relative stellar drift of 1 km/s turns the line by only about a ten-millionth of a radian per year. The rings near its midpoint drift about a tenth of an AU per year. The rings' Hall-effect thrusters absorb this continuously, so no ring is ever relocated in a single move.

Very rarely, a third star drifts close enough to a conduit's line that the conduit would have to pass where its __CIR__ cannot be sustained. The __RIC__ must then be split into two conduits, each terminating in the intruding star's system. A split is a major and costly undertaking, and an event that the traffic, economies, and governments of the affected systems must reckon with.

### Power Transmission

The isolation generators, station-keeping systems, control equipment, and other infrastructure aboard each ring require enormous quantities of energy.

This energy is transmitted along the __RIC__ using directed __gamma-ray power transmission__ and automatically-aligning receiver and transmitter dishes.

Critically, the gamma-ray beams themselves travel __within the isolated field of the RIC__, and the transmitting and receiving equipment responsible for relaying them is likewise maintained within that field. The power-transmission network therefore benefits from the same __CIR__ as the traffic travelling through the conduit, avoiding the otherwise-impractical light-speed delay that would occur if power had to propagate between rings through Typical Spacetime.

The power network is deliberately redundant. Under normal operation, each ring will generally receive and relay power through nearby rings along the conduit; however, transmitters and receivers are designed with sufficient steering authority and reserve range to bypass one or more neighbouring rings when required.

For example, if three consecutive rings—Ring A, Ring B, and Ring C—are operating normally, power may ordinarily be relayed from A to B and then from B to C. If Ring B suffers a failure that prevents it from maintaining the gamma-ray relay, Ring A may instead establish a temporary direct transmission to Ring C.

Such bypasses are ordinarily less efficient than the standard relay path due to their greater transmission distance, stricter alignment requirements, and potentially less favourable geometry, but they allow the power network to continue functioning while the affected ring is repaired.

This means the __RIC__ power network behaves less like a single serial chain and more like a sparse mesh of overlapping relay paths, with automatic control systems capable of reconfiguring transmission routes around damaged, unresponsive, or intentionally-isolated equipment.

Because gamma rays travel in straight lines, a bypass beam will not necessarily remain perfectly centred within the isolated field throughout its entire path. Depending on the geometry of the conduit, station-keeping errors, local field gradients, and the distance being bypassed, portions of the beam may briefly pass through regions of lower __CIR__ or even Typical Spacetime before re-entering the __RIC__.

This is not itself considered abnormal.

Even during routine operation, the boundary of a __RIC__ is not treated as an infinitely precise geometric surface. The overlapping fields produced by individual rings vary slightly in strength and geometry, and electromagnetic radiation travelling near the edges of the conduit may naturally pass through small variations in __CIR__.

Radiation does not become fundamentally different merely because it enters an isolated field. Rather, while travelling through an isolated region, its spatial progression is subject to the local __CIR__ in the same manner as any other moving phenomenon. A photon travelling through a high-__CIR__ region therefore covers distance more quickly relative to Typical Spacetime than the same photon travelling through a region with a lower __CIR__.

A beam which temporarily leaves the __RIC__ consequently experiences reduced effective spatial progression during that portion of its path before resuming the greater progression permitted by the conduit once it re-enters the isolated field.

The precise behaviour of electromagnetic radiation while crossing a sharp chronospatial boundary—including the effects on wavelength, frequency, energy, apparent direction, and other optical properties—is a distinct area of isolation physics and is not presently covered here.

The arrangement also requires specialised circuitry anywhere electrical or electromagnetic systems transition between regions experiencing significantly different __CIRs__.

A conventional conductor cannot simply be allowed to cross such a boundary. The electromagnetic fields, signals, and movement of charge within the conductor would be attempting to operate across two incompatible relationships between spatial and temporal progression, creating an unsafe chronospatial discontinuity.

Specialised chronospatial-interface circuitry is therefore used anywhere energy or information must cross between isolated and non-isolated systems.

Gamma-ray power transmission of this kind is commonplace throughout the settled systems of the Immediate Stellar Cluster, many of which contain extensive Dyson swarms and large interplanetary power grids, and the technology used by __RICs__ is primarily a particularly extreme application of an already-established power-transmission architecture.

Each ring additionally contains enough emergency battery storage to maintain itself for approximately __24 hours of IMT (ISCIA Mandated Time)__ following a loss of external power.

This endurance is generally sufficient for the transmission network to establish a bypass around the affected ring, for replacement power-transmission equipment to be installed, for a damaged relay to be repaired, or for another temporary power arrangement to be established before the ring can no longer safely maintain its required systems.

### Maintenance

Maintenance vehicles travel through the __RIC__ in the same manner as any other authorised ship.

Because a maintenance craft can use the conduit itself to reach a damaged or degraded ring, maintenance response times are determined primarily by travel through the __RIC__ rather than the many light-years that may separate the ring from either terminus in Typical Spacetime.

Maintenance craft commonly use high-thrust chemical propulsion and are designed to reach very high corridor velocities rapidly, whereas most ordinary traffic relies on much more efficient __MPD propulsion__ powered by on-board fusion generators.

When a maintenance vessel reaches the ring it has been assigned to, its autopilot will bring it alongside the installation and dock while both the ship and the ring remain isolated.

The maintenance crew cannot simply leave the ship and enter a section of the ring experiencing Typical Spacetime, however.

Unlike a spacecraft, each technician obviously does not carry a pocket-sized __HIIG-compatible isolation generator__ and a series of __IGICs__ capable of safely performing an impulse transition.

Instead, maintenance docks contain human-sized __synchronisation locks__.

Once technicians enter one of these locks, its __CIR__ is altered gradually over the course of several hours until the occupants have been safely returned to Typical Spacetime. The long transition time allows the chronospatial differential to be reduced slowly enough that the occupants never experience the extreme transition that would otherwise risk a CMCE and potentially fatal consequences.

The same process is performed in reverse when the crew finishes its work and needs to return to the still-isolated maintenance vessel.

### Construction

__RICs__ are constructed progressively, one segment and ring at a time.

A construction ship begins near an existing terminus and installs the first rings required to establish a short isolated section of conduit. Once operational, the construction vessel can use that completed portion of the __RIC__ to reach the next construction site more efficiently.

The process therefore becomes progressively easier as the conduit grows: every completed section provides the infrastructure required to reach and construct the next one.

Where practical, a __RIC__ will be constructed from both ends simultaneously. Construction fleets operating from each connected system progressively extend their respective sections toward one another until the two halves eventually meet, at which point the fields and physical infrastructure can be aligned and synchronised into a single continuous conduit.

## Synchronisation Lock (Arrival)

At the end of the __RIC__, another __synchronisation lock__ will await our arrival.

The aperture will already be open for our traffic group to enter, and our autopilot will guide the ship directly into its designated position within the lock.

The arrival lock is essentially the departure lock in reverse.

The __SLCT__ on this side will take control of the vessel, and our __IGICs__ will be charged if this was not already done during transit. Charging during the __RIC__ itself is often undesirable, as charging the __IGICs__ can require non-critical systems aboard the ship to be disabled momentarily. For this reason, the charging window upon arrival is generally somewhat more generous than the equivalent departure stage.

Once ready, our isolation drive will again enter __HIIG mode__, and the same precisely-timed interaction between the ship's isolation field and the lock itself will occur.

This time, however, the target is Typical Spacetime.

The ship will momentarily maintain a sufficiently compatible __CIR__ while the lock safely reduces its isolation from that of the __RIC__ back toward 1:1. Once the transition has been completed and the lock is fully reintegrated with Typical Spacetime, control of the ship will be returned to its crew.

The lock will open, and we can be on our way.

## Open RICs (ORICs)

__Open RICs (ORICs)__ are typically __RICs__ operating at relatively low __CIRs__, generally around __χ = 21 to 22 (roughly 1:1.3×10⁹ to 1:3.6×10⁹)__, within reach of capable ships' own isolation drives, which do __not__ require every ship to use a synchronisation lock.

Instead, there are two ways in and out of an __ORIC__:

- The standard __synchronisation lock__ used by conventional __RICs__.

- A free-use __open aperture__ that an authorised ship can approach and enter whenever required.

A ship using an __open aperture__ must be capable of generating and maintaining its own isolation field sufficiently close to the __RIC__'s current __CIR__ before attempting to cross the boundary.

The __SLCT__ will still authorise or deny any ship approaching the aperture, as a vessel remaining within Typical Spacetime could still trigger a CMCE if it attempted to cross directly into the conduit.

For this reason, the use of destructive force is often deployed more quickly around open apertures than around conventional synchronisation locks. Once a vessel is sufficiently close to crossing the boundary in an unsafe state, there may no longer be enough time to safely intercept it by any other means.

To effectively authorise and monitor ships approaching an open aperture, the __SLCT__ requires highly-specialised equipment capable of accurately observing and interacting with vessels that may already be operating within strong isolation fields.

This technology is comparatively recent and remains extremely expensive, which is why __ORICs__ are still much rarer than conventional __RICs__.

Despite this, early __ORICs__ have seen promising uptake.

Their lower typical __CIR__ means they cannot compete with high-performance conventional __RICs__ on raw transit time, but the ability for compatible ships to enter and leave without waiting for a scheduled synchronisation cycle provides vastly greater potential traffic frequency.

On sufficiently busy routes, this increased throughput has already allowed some __ORICs__ to demonstrate an economic advantage over conventional __RICs__ covering comparable distances.
