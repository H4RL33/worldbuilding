[[Spacecraft]] of the [[Immediate Stellar Cluster]] span more than a hundredfold range in length and some five orders of magnitude in mass. A vessel's size decides which [[Isolation#Sizes and Grades|isolation drive]] it carries, whether it can land, and how it moves between the stars.

## Classes

| Class | Length | Loaded mass | Reactor output | Main-engine acceleration | Drive size | Lands on an Earth-like world |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Light craft** (fighters, shuttles, scouts, personal transports) | 10–30 m | 20–150 t | ~20 kW/kg | ~0.08 g | 1 | yes |
| **Medium** (explorers, couriers, traders, gunships) | 30–80 m | 200–3,000 t | ~10 kW/kg | ~0.04 g | 2 | designs up to ~50 m |
| **Heavy ferries and landers** | 80–150 m | 3,000–15,000 t | ~4 kW/kg | ~0.016 g | 3 | yes, if built for it |
| **Frigates and regional freighters** | 150–400 m | 20,000–300,000 t | ~1.5 kW/kg | ~0.006 g | 3 | no |
| **Capital ships** (destroyers, cruisers, carriers) | 400 m–1 km | 0.3–2 million t | ~1 kW/kg | ~0.004 g | 4 | no |
| **Battleships and trunk freighters** | 1–1.5 km | 1–3 million t | ~0.6 kW/kg | ~0.0024 g | 4 | no |

The largest vessels need on the order of two terawatts of generation and usually carry several large stellarators (see [[Spacecraft Propulsion]]).

## Landing

A vessel's thrust grows with the area of its engines and intakes, while its mass grows with its volume. The largest vessel able to land therefore shrinks as a world's gravity grows.

- **Earth-like gravity:** general-purpose craft up to about 50 m can land, and purpose-built heavy ferries up to about 150 m.
- **About 0.4 g:** both limits are roughly two and a half times larger.
- **Airless worlds:** air-breathing engines cannot help, and craft using high-energy chemical propulsion are the specialists.

## Conduit Clearance

To pass through a [[RIC]], a vessel must clear its rings. The [[ITA]] standard limits any vessel intending to use a RIC to **400 m across**. Shipbuilders seldom accept a smaller market, so the largest classes grow longer instead of wider, and trunk freighters and battleships alike are long, slender hulls.

## Typical Operations

An independent crew typically lives aboard a medium-to-large vessel, which stays in orbit while a docked light personal transport carries them to and from the surface.

Navigation computers plan these trips in full, from the larger vessel's approach and parking to the transport's departure, the moment to collapse its field and a safe descent corridor, so a crew is rarely kept waiting.

A vessel that lands where it cannot take off again is not lost outright. Its crew can lighten it until it can lift, leave in its docked transport or call one of the rescue services that answer distress beacons. The grounded hull is then salvaged where it lies, converted into a surface installation or written off by its insurers. Many such hulls have become landmarks in their own right.

Light craft carry size-1 drives without the capacitor banks needed for a high-impulse isolation transition. They cannot enter a RIC and are effectively bound to their home system, so they travel between the stars only aboard larger vessels such as carriers and transports.

Trunk freighters are economically analogous to ULCVs. They link a small number of major centres through RICs and mostly serve stations and industry in space. Moving their cargo to and from a world's surface is slow by comparison: unloading two million tonnes takes several hundred heavy-ferry trips. Ferry fleets and orbital transfer stations are therefore central to every major port.
