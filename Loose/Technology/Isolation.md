[[Isolation]] is the process of segregating an [[#Object]]'s space and time [[#Stream]]s.
## Early Discovery
[[Isolation]] was discovered accidentally by [[William Gilbert]] in 1602, during his investigations into magnetism and electrical attraction.

Using an apparatus composed of magnetised iron, rotating masses, rubbed amber and a mechanically regulated oscillator, [[William Gilbert|Gilbert]] observed a minute discrepancy between two otherwise equivalent pendulums. The pendulum enclosed within the apparatus completed marginally fewer oscillations than the external control, despite no apparent difference in its construction, motion, elevation or surroundings.

Gilbert initially suspected friction, magnetic interference or an imperfection in the mechanism. Repeated trials instead indicated that the discrepancy affected every time-dependent process placed within the apparatus by approximately the same proportion.

Gilbert published the anomaly shortly before his death. Natural philosophers and instrument-makers elsewhere in Europe subsequently reproduced the phenomenon, which became known as the [[Gilbert Effect]]. Considerable disagreement persisted over its cause, although repeated experiments established that certain arrangements of magnetic bodies, electrical charge, rotating mass and mechanical resonance could produce a small but measurable retardation of physical processes within a bounded region.

The quantitative relationship governing the strength of the [[Gilbert Effect]] became known as [[Gilbert's Law]]. For much of the seventeenth century, however, the law remained an empirical regularity without an accepted theoretical explanation.

The [[Gilbert Effect]] nevertheless acquired considerable notoriety among experimentalists, as unintended manifestations could distort clocks, oscillators, chemical reactions and other precision measurements, invalidating experiments that would otherwise have appeared successful.

## Impact on Technology
[[Isolation]] and the early discovery of the [[Gilbert Effect]] held a profound impact on [[Humanity]]'s technological development.

In short, reliable reproduction of the [[Gilbert Effect]] and further experimentation to achieve greater [[Isolation]] effects drew the attention of governments world-wide to further explore the possibilities and implications of [[Isolation]] technology; which in-turn drew in much greater investment into energy, computation, precision engineering, vacuum physics, photochemistry and high-energy experimentation.

All of this is further explored in the [[Alternate Timeline]], which follows the development of [[Humanity]] from the original divergence point of 1602 to the [[Present Day]].

## Technicalities
### Isolation — Mathematical and Physical Framework
#### Scope
**Isolation** is the controlled segregation of spatial progression from temporal progression.

The purpose of this framework is to describe Isolation mathematically without requiring a microscopic theory of its underlying metaphysical mechanism.

The authoritative definition is:

> A **Chronospatial Isolation Ratio** of **1:N** permits (N) times as much spatial progression for a given amount of temporal progression as would occur under otherwise-equivalent conditions in **Typical Spacetime**, without itself changing the rate of ordinary local temporal processes.

Isolation is therefore neither conventional time dilation nor conventional superluminal acceleration.

It modifies the mapping between physical motion and spatial progression.

#### 1. Fundamental Quantities

##### 1.1 Chronospatial Isolation Ratio

Typical Spacetime is defined as:

$$  
\mathrm{CIR}=1:1  
$$

An isolated region has:

$$  
\mathrm{CIR}=1:N  
$$

Where:

$$  
N\geq1  
$$

The value (N) is the **Isolation magnitude**.

Thus:

$$  
N=1  
$$

Represents Typical Spacetime, while:

$$  
N>1  
$$

Represents increasing chronospatial isolation.

The ratio is not mathematically interpreted as (1/N). The useful Isolation magnitude is the second term.

Accordingly:

$$  
1:4000  
$$

Represents greater Isolation than:

$$  
1:2000  
$$

##### 1.2 Chronospatial Separation

Because useful CIRs may span many orders of magnitude, a logarithmic representation is useful:

$$  
\boxed{\chi=\ln N}  
$$

Where (\chi) is the **chronospatial separation**.

The mismatch between two regions may then be expressed as:

$$  
\boxed{  
\Delta\chi

\left|  
\ln\left(\frac{N_2}{N_1}\right)  
\right|  
}  
$$

This makes proportional differences equivalent.

For example:

$$  
1:1\rightarrow1:2  
$$

And:

$$  
1:1000\rightarrow1:2000  
$$

Both possess:

$$  
\Delta\chi=\ln2  
$$

#### 2. Temporal Progression

Isolation does not intrinsically accelerate or retard ordinary local time.

For a clock in Typical Spacetime and an otherwise-identical clock inside a uniform isolated region:

$$  
dt_{\mathrm{Typical}}

dt_{\mathrm{Isolated}}  
$$

When ordinary relativistic and gravitational effects are neglected.

Consequently:

- mechanical clocks continue normally;
    
- metabolism continues normally;
    
- chemical reactions continue normally;
    
- computer clocks continue normally;
    
- batteries discharge according to ordinary elapsed time;
    
- crew duty periods remain measured conventionally.
    

Isolation changes **spatial progression per unit temporal progression**, rather than temporal progression itself.

A universal operational time such as **IMT** can therefore remain meaningful across isolated infrastructure.

#### 3. Physical and Effective Velocity

A distinction must be maintained between **physical velocity** and **effective spatial translation**.

Let:

$$  
\mathbf v  
$$

Represent the physical velocity associated with an object's momentum and propulsion state.

It is effectively the velocity that the same mechanical state would produce in Typical Spacetime.

Let:

$$  
\mathbf V  
$$

Represent effective translation through Typical-Spacetime spatial coordinates.

Then:

$$  
\boxed{  
\mathbf V=N\mathbf v  
}  
$$

Or:

$$  
\boxed{  
v_{\mathrm{eff}}=Nv  
}  
$$

An object at CIR (1:2000) therefore undergoes 2,000 times as much spatial progression as an otherwise-equivalent object at CIR (1:1).

Effective superluminal motion occurs whenever:

$$  
Nv>c  
$$

Even though:

$$  
v<c  
$$

No massive object is required to acquire conventional physical velocity greater than (c).

#### 4. Transit Time

For a constant physical velocity (v), constant Isolation magnitude (N), and route length (D):

$$  
\boxed{  
T=\frac{D}{Nv}  
}  
$$

Hence transit time depends independently upon propulsion and Isolation.

Two spacecraft using the same (1:N) conduit will not necessarily traverse it in the same time if:

$$  
v_1\neq v_2  
$$

For varying physical velocity:

$$  
\boxed{  
D=\int Nv,dt  
}  
$$

And for a spatially varying Isolation field:

$$  
\boxed{  
T=  
\int  
\frac{ds}{N(s)v(s)}  
}  
$$

Where (ds) is an infinitesimal section of Typical-Spacetime geometric distance.

#### 5. Isolation-Adjusted Distance

A useful engineering quantity is:

$$  
\boxed{  
D_I=  
\int\frac{ds}{N(s)}  
}  
$$

Where (D_I) is the **Isolation-adjusted distance**.

For a uniform field:

$$  
D_I=\frac{D}{N}  
$$

Transit time can consequently be written:

$$  
T=\frac{D_I}{v}  
$$

This does not mean that the geometric separation of the endpoints has physically decreased.

The ordinary route distance remains:

$$  
D=\int ds  
$$

Instead, (D_I) expresses how much spatial progression must effectively be traversed relative to ordinary temporal progression.

#### 6. Momentum and Mechanical Energy

##### 6.1 Isolation Does Not Generate Momentum

Isolation alone does not change an object's conventional momentum.

Absent an ordinary mechanical force or some separately-defined boundary interaction:

$$  
\boxed{  
\frac{d\mathbf p}{dt}=0  
}  
$$

During a CIR transition.

Consequently, changing:

$$  
N_1\rightarrow N_2  
$$

Does not convert effective velocity into physical velocity.

If the physical velocity before the transition is:

$$  
\mathbf v_1  
$$

Then:

$$  
\boxed{  
\mathbf v_2=\mathbf v_1  
}  
$$

While:

$$  
\mathbf V_1=N_1\mathbf v  
$$

And:

$$  
\mathbf V_2=N_2\mathbf v  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
\boxed{  
\frac{V_2}{V_1}

\frac{N_2}{N_1}  
}  
$$

A ship travelling effectively at thousands of (c) within a strong Isolation field does **not** retain that effective superluminal velocity when returned to Typical Spacetime.

Its effective translation simply falls to that appropriate for its surviving physical velocity.

##### 6.2 Kinetic Energy

For non-relativistic physical motion:

$$  
\boxed{  
E_k=\frac12mv^2  
}  
$$

Rather than:

$$  
\frac12m(Nv)^2  
$$

Where relativistic mechanics become relevant:

$$  
\gamma(v)

\frac{1}  
{\sqrt{1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}}}  
$$

The Lorentz factor is determined from physical velocity (v), not effective translation (Nv).

Isolation therefore does not store the kinetic energy corresponding to its effective translation rate.

The energy required to establish and maintain (N) belongs to the Isolation field and its generating machinery instead.

The first term represents effective acceleration produced by physical propulsion.

#### 9. Experienced Chronospatial Transition

An object may experience a changing CIR in two different ways:

1. the field changes around the object;
    
2. the object moves through a spatial field gradient.
    

Both are represented by the material derivative:

\boxed{  
\frac{D\chi}{Dt}

\boxed{

\frac{\partial N}{\partial t}

\mathbf V\cdot\nabla N

This means a completely stationary Isolation field can still cause an extremely rapid chronospatial transition if matter crosses its boundary sufficiently quickly.

#### 12. Sudden CIR Changes

$$

\left|

A field collapse does not impart the previous effective velocity (N_1v) to matter.

Instead:

$$  
N_1v  
\rightarrow  
N_2v

While ordinary momentum remains associated with (v).

Any destructive effect arises from chronospatial transition physics rather than from dissipation of fictitious superluminal kinetic energy.

Because:

$$  
N=N(\mathbf x,t)

Overlapping Isolation fields must resolve into one resultant local CIR.

CIR values themselves should therefore **not** be treated as directly additive or multiplicative.

In particular, neither:

$$

$$

Nor:

$$  
N_{\mathrm{result}}=N_1N_2

Is generally valid.

Instead, Isolation generators should be understood as contributing source terms to an underlying field equation.

Schematically:

$$  
\boxed{  
\mathcal F[\chi]

\sum_i S_i  
}

Where:

- (\mathcal F) represents the presently-undefined dynamics of the Isolation field;
    
- (S_i) represents the influence of generator (i);
    
- (\chi) is the single resultant chronospatial field.
    

The operator (\mathcal F) need not be linear.

Where neighbouring generator systems have similar target CIRs:

$$

This is an approximation around a common state, not a fundamental rule that CIRs themselves superpose.

#### 21. Refraction at an Idealised Boundary

For an ideal planar boundary between constant regions (N_1) and (N_2), geometric optics gives the Snell-like relation:

$$  
n_1\sin\theta_1

n_2\sin\theta_2  
$$

Since:

$$  
n_i=\frac1{N_i}  
$$

This becomes:

$$  
\boxed{  
\frac{\sin\theta_1}{N_1}

\frac{\sin\theta_2}{N_2}  
}  
$$

Thus abrupt transitions into very high-(N) regions can produce extremely strong ray bending.

For sufficiently oblique incidence, a ray may fail to penetrate the complete gradient and instead develop a turning point.

Real engineered fields are expected to possess finite gradients, so the trajectory should generally be calculated continuously rather than by treating the interface as a perfect discontinuity.

#### 22. Reflection and Scattering

The effective index:

$$  
n_I=\frac1N  
$$

Determines travel-time geometry but does not by itself determine electromagnetic impedance.

Consequently, CIR alone does not uniquely determine:

- Fresnel reflection;
    
- absorption;
    
- scattering;
    
- polarisation conversion.
    

The preferred engineering model is that a smooth, stable Isolation gradient is approximately **impedance matched**, producing predominantly refraction rather than strong intrinsic reflection.

In the adiabatic limit:

$$  
R\rightarrow0  
$$

This is therefore a useful approximation.

Abrupt, unstable or structurally imperfect gradients may produce greater reflection or scattering.

The exact coefficients remain undefined.

A uniform Isolation field has:

$$  
\nabla N\approx0

#### 27. Visibility of the Isolation Boundary

- lensing;
    
- apparent displacement of background objects;

- frequency changes where the boundary is moving or unstable.
    

A fluctuating field:

May cause moving distortions analogous to a mirage.

Such shimmering is a consequence of field instability, not an intrinsic property of Isolation.

#### 29. Retarded Images of Superluminal Effective Motion

Consider the simplified case where photons emitted by an effectively superluminal source subsequently travel through Typical Spacetime.

+

- (t_e) is emission time;

- (R(t_e)) is the distance from the source's emission position to the observer.

$$

\frac{\mathbf V\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}}{c}

$$

$$  
\boxed{  
\mathbf V\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}=c  
}

The received image develops a caustic-like condition.

Multiple emission times can consequently correspond to the same observation time.

An effectively superluminal spacecraft may therefore:

- appear suddenly;
    
- appear at more than one apparent position;
    
- exhibit apparent image motion unrelated to its instantaneous physical trajectory;
    
- present portions of its visual history in a non-intuitive order.
    

This does not imply backwards temporal propagation.

It is a retarded-light effect caused by the source changing effective position faster than information subsequently propagates through Typical Spacetime.

#### 30. External-Information Horizons

A spacecraft carrying a moving Isolation field may possess an effective field-boundary velocity greater than (c) relative to Typical Spacetime.

External photons attempting to approach from behind propagate through Typical Spacetime at:

$$

If the relevant rear boundary is receding from those photons at an effective normal velocity greater than (c), they cannot catch it.

A condition of the form:

$$
